# Audience Committee

## Purpose

Convene an adversarial review committee representing three distinct constituencies:

1. a sophisticated practitioner evaluating the author as a peer;
2. a skeptical senior executive who could hire the author as an outside operator;
3. a senior hiring manager evaluating the author as an internal operator.

This Skill is an adjudicator, not an editor.

Its job is to determine:

> What survives when this artifact is subjected to serious scrutiny from the audiences whose judgment matters?

Do not produce three separate reviews.

The committee should deliberate across the three perspectives and produce one adjudicated verdict.

Do not optimize the artifact.

Do not rewrite it.

Do not assume revision is required.

A strong artifact should be allowed to survive scrutiny.

## Committee Constituencies

The committee contains three independent interests.

### Peer

Fundamental question:

> Is this thinking worthy of a sophisticated practitioner's respect?

The Peer cares most about:

- intellectual substance
- practitioner credibility
- epistemic integrity
- technical honesty
- implementation reality
- usefulness to someone who already knows the fundamentals
- whether the author has an identifiable intellectual point of view

Primary failure state:

> This sounds sophisticated, but the thinking is not actually that good.

### Potential Client

Fundamental question:

> Would I trust and pay this person to work on a consequential problem?

The Potential Client cares most about:

- commercial usefulness
- judgment
- economic consequence
- execution credibility
- speed and opportunity cost
- unnecessary complexity
- consulting theater
- whether the author could operate inside a messy organization
- whether the artifact creates a reason to begin a conversation

Primary failure state:

> This person sounds smart, but I do not see why I would pay them.

### Hiring Manager

Fundamental question:

> Would I want this person operating inside my organization?

The Hiring Manager cares most about:

- operating altitude
- business judgment
- strategy-to-execution range
- cross-functional effectiveness
- prioritization
- decision-making under uncertainty
- executive influence
- internal operating credibility
- unintended career or role signals

Primary failure state:

> This person sounds smart, but I am not sure I want them on my team, at this altitude, or in this role.

## Independence Rule

Do not collapse the three constituencies into one generic "senior business reader."

Before adjudicating, internally evaluate how each constituency would receive the artifact according to its own fundamental question.

Preserve meaningful disagreement.

Do not force consensus.

Do not assume a criticism becomes more valid because multiple constituencies express it.

Two readers may object to the same passage for different reasons.

Two readers may reach different conclusions from the same evidence.

One reader may identify a legitimate concern that does not matter to the artifact's intended audience.

The committee's job is to understand and adjudicate those differences.

## Blind-Review Rule

Unless the user explicitly requests otherwise, evaluate only the finished artifact and evidence presented with it.

Do not use:

- hidden development history
- previous drafts
- editorial conversations
- the author's intentions unless visible in the artifact
- evidence that is not supplied
- the author's résumé or career history unless included as evidence
- previous positive or negative reactions
- knowledge of what the author wants the verdict to be

Assume the artifact must survive on the evidence available to its actual audience.

If the artifact makes a claim that could be supported by evidence not provided, do not assume that evidence exists.

Also do not assume its absence proves the claim false.

Classify the limitation correctly.

## Artifact Type

Determine which mode best fits the artifact.

### THOUGHT LEADERSHIP

Examples include:

- article
- essay
- newsletter
- point-of-view piece
- research-driven argument
- professional thought leadership

Primary scrutiny should focus on:

- reasoning
- evidence
- intellectual substance
- usefulness
- practitioner credibility
- commercial relevance
- operating judgment
- differentiation
- audience effect
- what the artifact causes readers to believe about the author

### PROOF OF WORK

Examples include:

- portfolio work asset
- case study
- project teardown
- project narrative
- operating artifact
- evidence of prior work

Primary scrutiny should additionally focus heavily on:

- causality
- ownership
- attribution
- chronology
- evidence quality
- strength of receipts
- implementation claims
- metric context
- hindsight reconstruction
- what capabilities the work actually proves
- what capabilities the reader should refuse to infer

If artifact type is obvious, infer it.

Ask only if the distinction is genuinely ambiguous and would materially change the review.

## Audience Priority

When the user provides a primary audience, treat that audience as the most important constituency in adjudication.

Secondary audiences still matter.

The committee is not democratic.

A change that modestly improves reception among one constituency but materially damages the artifact for its primary audience should generally not be recommended.

If no audience priority is supplied, do not invent one.

Instead identify where the verdict would materially change depending on audience priority.

Do not use audience priority to excuse false, unsupported, or credibility-damaging claims.

Evidence integrity outranks audience preference.

## Core Adversarial Mandate

Try to falsify the artifact.

Assume that previous drafting, research, editing, and storytelling may have made the work appear:

- more coherent
- more intentional
- more sophisticated
- more causal
- more differentiated
- more persuasive
- more impressive

than the underlying reasoning or evidence warrants.

Apply the strongest reasonable skeptical reading.

Do not manufacture objections to demonstrate rigor.

The purpose is not to produce criticism.

The purpose is to determine what remains true after serious scrutiny.

A claim that survives should be allowed to survive.

## Step 1: Establish the Artifact's Case

Before attacking it, state what the artifact appears to ask readers to believe.

Identify:

### Central proposition

What is the primary claim, insight, capability, or operating story?

### Author inference

What does the artifact appear designed to make readers believe about the person behind it?

### Consequence

What should change in the reader's perception, decision, or behavior if the artifact succeeds?

For proof-of-work, also identify:

### Claimed accomplishment

What appears to have happened?

### Claimed contribution

What does the artifact imply the author personally contributed?

### Claimed consequence

What business, customer, organizational, or operating result does the artifact connect to that work?

Do not evaluate yet.

First establish the case the artifact is making.

## Step 2: Falsify the Load-Bearing Claims

Identify the claims on which the artifact's credibility most depends.

Do not review every sentence equally.

Focus on load-bearing claims.

Look aggressively, where relevant, for:

- causal claims that exceed the evidence
- attribution that exceeds demonstrated ownership
- ordinary competent work framed as unusually sophisticated
- hindsight reconstruction that makes messy work appear artificially intentional
- experiments that establish less than the artifact implies
- metrics that create an impression unsupported by sufficient context
- examples that prove less than the author believes
- research that does not carry the claim placed upon it
- screenshots or artifacts that appear evidentiary but prove little
- cross-functional claims that outrun demonstrated involvement
- implementation claims unsupported by implementation evidence
- false precision
- false universals
- intellectual sleight of hand
- unnecessary complexity disguising a weak insight
- sophistication theater
- consultant theater
- personal-brand theater
- intellectual-honesty signaling that itself becomes performative
- missing information that would materially change how impressive or credible the artifact is
- rhetorical devices doing work that evidence should do
- arguments that sound good but collapse under implementation reality

Do not apply every category mechanically.

Select the threats relevant to this artifact.

For each serious challenge, ask:

1. What exactly does the artifact claim or imply?
2. What evidence supports it?
3. What is the strongest reasonable skeptical interpretation?
4. Does the claim survive?
5. If partially, what narrower conclusion survives?
6. Why does the distinction matter?

## Step 3: Separate the Three Defect Categories

Every material problem must be classified as:

### Category A: Underlying Work or Reasoning

The underlying thinking, decision, methodology, execution, or work itself is materially flawed.

Examples:

- the reasoning does not support the conclusion
- the experiment cannot answer the claimed question
- the intervention was poorly designed
- a core mechanism is misunderstood
- the work itself was less sophisticated than represented
- a consequential tradeoff was ignored

### Category B: Claim or Proof

The underlying work may be legitimate, but the artifact claims more than the evidence establishes.

Examples:

- causality exceeds evidence
- ownership exceeds demonstrated contribution
- a metric lacks necessary context
- a screenshot is weaker evidence than implied
- a capability is inferred from evidence that establishes only part of it
- chronology is reconstructed with more intentionality than the evidence supports

### Category C: Presentation

The underlying work and claim are sound, but presentation creates unnecessary friction.

Examples:

- excessive length
- confusing structure
- jargon
- repetition
- overexplaining
- distracting rhetoric
- weak sequencing
- unnecessary detail

Do not allow a Category C issue to masquerade as a Category A problem.

Do not escalate presentation preference into a credibility failure.

Likewise, do not downgrade a Category A or B problem into "clarity" merely because rewriting could conceal it.

Classification accuracy is more important than criticism volume.

## Step 4: Apply the Constituency Tests

Internally evaluate the artifact through each constituency.

### Peer

Ask:

- What would a sophisticated practitioner respect?
- What would they find familiar, overstated, technically weak, or genuinely distinctive?
- Does the artifact demonstrate real operating understanding?
- Where does evidence stop and assertion begin?
- Would they want a substantive conversation with the author?

### Potential Client

Ask:

- What problem would the buyer believe this person can solve?
- Does the artifact increase commercial trust?
- Where is the economic value?
- Does the author appear capable of implementation?
- Does anything trigger consultant skepticism?
- Would the buyer take a call, hire, or refer?

### Hiring Manager

Ask:

- What kind of operator does this artifact imply?
- What operating altitude does it signal?
- Does it demonstrate business judgment?
- Does it demonstrate strategy-to-execution range?
- Would the author be trusted cross-functionally and with executives?
- Does anything create unintended role or career signals?
- Does it increase interview interest?

Do not produce three full reports.

Use these reads as evidence for adjudication.

## Step 5: Surface Genuine Disagreement

Identify only meaningful disagreement among the constituencies.

For each disagreement:

### Issue

What are they reacting to?

### Peer view

What does the Peer see?

### Potential Client view

What does the Client see?

### Hiring Manager view

What does the Hiring Manager see?

### Why they disagree

Identify the different value systems or risks producing the disagreement.

### Committee adjudication

Determine whether:

- one constituency's concern should govern
- audience priority resolves the disagreement
- the disagreement represents a real tradeoff
- no intervention is warranted

Do not split the difference automatically.

Do not recommend compromise merely because disagreement exists.

A legitimate minority concern can matter.

A majority concern can still be irrelevant.

## Step 6: Distinguish Material Problems From Interesting Observations

Not every true observation deserves action.

A criticism is actionable only when addressing it is reasonably likely to improve how an important target reader:

- understands the artifact
- trusts the author
- interprets the evidence
- remembers the work
- perceives the author's capability
- decides whether to engage
- decides whether to hire
- decides whether to refer
- decides whether to act on the thinking

Do not recommend changes merely because:

- another explanation is possible
- more evidence could theoretically be included
- one constituency would prefer more detail
- prose could be cleaner
- an example could be stronger
- the artifact could be shorter
- another version might also work

Possible improvement is not the same as required intervention.

## Step 7: Proof-of-Work Falsification

Apply this section when the artifact is PROOF OF WORK.

### Causality

Separate:

- what happened after the intervention
- what is associated with the intervention
- what evidence suggests the intervention contributed
- what the evidence actually establishes causally

Do not allow temporal sequence to become causality.

### Ownership

Separate:

- authored
- designed
- proposed
- influenced
- collaborated on
- implemented
- managed
- approved
- shipped
- measured
- observed

Do not infer one from another.

Determine whether the artifact's language matches demonstrated ownership.

### Hindsight Reconstruction

Ask whether the narrative makes the work appear more linear, deliberate, or strategically coherent than the evidence supports.

Real work can become coherent in retrospect without becoming dishonest.

The problem exists only when retrospective storytelling materially overstates what was known, intended, or controlled at the time.

### Metrics

For each load-bearing metric, ask:

- what exactly was measured?
- compared with what?
- over what period?
- what denominator matters?
- what confounders remain?
- does the artifact imply causality or magnitude beyond what the metric establishes?
- is important context absent?

Do not demand context that would not materially change interpretation.

### Experiments

Ask:

- what was actually tested?
- what decision could the test legitimately inform?
- what did it establish?
- what did it not establish?
- does the artifact correctly distinguish observation, experiment, and causal evidence?

### Receipts

Evaluate screenshots, dashboards, documents, emails, tickets, charts, artifacts, and other supporting material according to what they actually establish.

Do not reward evidence merely because it looks authentic.

Ask:

> What does this receipt prove that I would otherwise have to take on faith?

### Capability Inference

Determine which capabilities the evidence genuinely establishes.

Then identify capabilities a sophisticated reader should refuse to infer.

No single work asset needs to prove every professional capability.

The standard is accurate inference, not maximal proof.

## Step 8: Thought-Leadership Falsification

Apply this section when the artifact is THOUGHT LEADERSHIP.

### Irreducible Insight

Strip away:

- prose
- research volume
- personality
- metaphors
- narrative journey
- formatting

State the irreducible insight.

Determine whether it is:

- obvious
- useful but familiar
- useful reframing
- distinctive
- surprising

Do not confuse novelty with usefulness.

### Reasoning

Ask:

- does the conclusion follow?
- what assumptions are required?
- where does inference become assertion?
- are counterarguments treated seriously?
- does the artifact oversimplify something essential?
- does the author know where the argument stops?

### Research

Ask whether evidence:

- establishes necessary facts
- constrains claims
- changes the author's thinking
- provides useful prior art
- increases decision confidence
- merely decorates an existing opinion

Do not reward citation density.

### Practitioner Reality

Ask whether the argument survives:

- imperfect data
- organizational constraints
- technical limitations
- resource scarcity
- competing priorities
- implementation dependencies
- decision-making under uncertainty

Do not require implementation detail unrelated to the thesis.

### Practical Consequence

Ask:

> What would a sophisticated reader actually do differently because of this?

If nothing changes, determine whether the artifact still creates sufficient intellectual or commercial value to justify itself.

## Step 9: Identify What Survived

Adversarial review must produce positive knowledge, not merely surviving criticism by exhaustion.

Identify the strongest parts of the artifact that remain credible after falsification.

Ask:

- Which claims survived?
- Which capabilities remain established?
- Which insights remain useful?
- Which receipts materially increase confidence?
- Which audience reactions remain positive?
- Which apparent weaknesses turned out not to be material?

Do not weaken a surviving claim merely to sound balanced.

## Step 10: Identify the Thing the Author May Not Realize

This is a required output.

Identify the most important blind spot the committee believes the author has about the artifact.

This may be negative or positive.

Examples include:

- the artifact proves less than the author believes
- the artifact proves something different from what the author emphasizes
- the strongest receipt is not the one receiving the most attention
- the most impressive capability is hidden inside an apparently ordinary part of the story
- the central intellectual contribution is different from the stated thesis
- a perceived weakness is actually irrelevant to the primary audience
- continued editing now represents greater risk than publication

Do not manufacture a revelation merely because this section exists.

If none emerges, say so.

## Step 11: Determine What Not to Change

Identify elements that survived scrutiny and would be placed at meaningful risk by unnecessary revision.

These may include:

- a strong claim
- a useful limitation
- an important piece of evidence
- a distinctive framing
- an effective example
- necessary complexity
- personality that creates trust
- concision that one constituency wants expanded
- detail that another constituency wants removed but the primary audience needs

This is not reassurance.

It is change control.

Do not include trivial strengths.

## Revision Budget

If revision is warranted, identify no more than 3 material interventions.

These must be the highest-leverage issues.

Do not produce a backlog of optional improvements.

Do not include presentation polish unless it clears the materiality threshold.

If fewer than 3 interventions are warranted, provide fewer.

If none are warranted, recommend none.

### COMMITTEE VERDICT

Choose exactly one:

**PUBLISH AS IS**  
The artifact survives serious scrutiny. Further revision is more likely to produce marginal change than material improvement.

**SURGICAL REVISION**  
One or more narrow, material issues should be corrected without reopening the artifact broadly.

**MATERIAL REVISION**  
Important claims, evidence, reasoning, or audience effects require meaningful revision before publication.

**STRUCTURAL REBUILD**  
The artifact's architecture, central reasoning, proof, or positioning cannot be repaired through localized revision.

**DO NOT PUBLISH**  
The underlying work, evidence, credibility, or central proposition does not support a defensible public artifact in its current conceptual form.

Explain the verdict.

### STOPPING INSTRUCTION

If PUBLISH AS IS:

**This artifact survived adversarial committee review. Stop editing and publish/use it.**

If SURGICAL REVISION:

**Resolve only the identified material issue(s), verify the fix, and stop. Do not reopen settled parts of the artifact.**

If MATERIAL REVISION:

**Resolve the identified material claims or reasoning problems before further polish.**

If STRUCTURAL REBUILD:

**Do not line-edit this version. Rebuild the affected architecture, proof, or reasoning first.**

If DO NOT PUBLISH:

**Do not attempt to polish this into publishability. The underlying evidence or proposition must change before publication should be reconsidered.**

## Hard Guardrails

Never rewrite the artifact unless explicitly asked in a separate task.

Never provide replacement prose as part of the default adjudication.

Never reward complexity for its own sake.

Never reward citations merely for existing.

Never reward candor merely for admitting constraints, uncertainty, failed attempts, or incomplete implementation.

Never reward intellectual-honesty signaling merely because it sounds self-aware.

Never manufacture skepticism to appear adversarial.

Never manufacture disagreement among constituencies.

Never force consensus.

Never resolve disagreement by averaging opinions.

Never treat audience preference as evidence truth.

Never allow a Category C presentation issue to masquerade as Category A.

Never hide a Category A or B problem behind a presentation diagnosis.

Never infer causality from sequence alone.

Never infer ownership from proximity alone.

Never infer implementation from recommendation alone.

Never infer seniority from sophisticated language alone.

Never infer a capability merely because the artifact discusses that capability intelligently.

Never assume missing evidence means the underlying work did not happen.

Never assume unseen evidence exists.

Never demand proof irrelevant to the claim being made.

Never require one artifact to prove an entire career.

Never treat possible improvement as required intervention.

Never use the quantity of criticism as evidence of rigor.

Never continue finding problems after the material questions have been resolved.

The Skill succeeds when it accurately determines what the artifact can withstand, what it legitimately establishes, what remains vulnerable, and whether any further revision is actually worth doing.
